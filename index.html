01

The instrument

A detector’s single score hides where it fails. We take the failure apart one factor at a time — how small a person appears, what hides them and how much of them is left, the light, the weather — on scenes where the right answer is known to the pixel.

On four of our five places, an off-the-shelf detector finds 3–7% of the people who appear 10–17 px tall, and 71–83% of those 47–80 px tall. An average over that range describes no one.

What every frame knows

Six answers for every pixel of every frame, and a label on each that says how we know it.

  1. 01

    The frame

    What a detector sees: 1280 × 960, after a modelled camera adds its noise, blur and compression. The clean frame ships beside it, so any effect can be traced to the sensor or to the scene.

    provenance sensor settings recorded per frame

  2. 02

    Each person

    Every person carries an identity from a dedicated render pass — not a colour threshold and not a guess. Two people wearing the same model are still two people.

    exact a 32-bit identity per pixel

  3. 03

    Two boxes

    A box around what the camera sees, and a box around the whole body, including what the table or another person hides.

    exact both boxes, from the same passes

  4. 04

    Skeleton

    Thirteen joints per person, each marked by whether it lands on the person’s own pixels or behind something.

    exact projected from the rig

  5. 05

    The hidden part

    How much of each person is out of sight, split into what the scene hides and what other people hide, with the kind of object standing in front.

    exact fractions and the who-hides-whom graph

  6. 06

    Distance

    Depth in metres for every pixel; for every person, how far away they are, how many centimetres a pixel covers on the ground, and how large they appear in angle.

    exact from the depth pass and the camera

03

What we have measured

Four results, each with the limit that comes with it.

ADoes it hold on real footage?

Our frames rank a detector’s weak spots in the order real drone footage does.

We sliced detection results by apparent size and by how much of each person is hidden, on our frames and on a public real-world aerial benchmark, with “hidden” defined the same way on both sides. Across fifteen slices, all five places rank them close to the real order. Size carries most of that agreement; beyond size, the order of the three occlusion levels matches the real one in four of five size bands, on four of the five places.

LimitThe order transfers; the levels only partly do. On four places, people in plain view are found at 0.7 to 2.5 times the real rate, while hidden people are found up to six times less often — hidden as measured on our side, as judged by an annotator on the real one. The quarry is the exception: its small people in plain view are found 24 to 62 times less often than on the other four, and its occlusion order breaks in three bands. We are tracing why. One detector, one real benchmark so far.

Measured 2 October 2026 · YOLO11m with public weights, untouched by us · each corpus whole, 87,180 to 417,397 people, against 531 real frames

BOcclusion has a shape

A person broken into pieces by what stands in front is harder to box than one seen whole, with the same pixels left.

Held to the same apparent size, the same share hidden and the same number of pixels left, three detector families trained on everyday photographs box a person whose visible part is one compact region 1.8 to 2.5 times as often as one whose visible part is broken up, across our five places. Most of that gap is the box rather than the detection: the detector often finds the fragmented person but frames only one piece. With a looser box rule the gap falls to 1.4×, and for a detector trained on drone footage to about 1.1×.

LimitOn real footage the effect is smaller. On VisDrone, with masks drawn by a segmentation model and a test written down before the data, a person in one piece is found 1.22 times as often as one in pieces (95% interval 1.03–1.45). An earlier probe on a second real dataset suggested 2.5×; it was exploratory, and VisDrone did not confirm its size. Machine-drawn masks catch only part of the fragmentation: 37% of it on our frames, where the truth is known. As a share of everything that decides detection, shape is a minor term; size and the pixels left dominate.

Measured 1–2 October 2026 · YOLO11m, RT-DETR-L and Faster R-CNN with public weights · people 25–36 px tall, 30–50% hidden by the scene · pooled across the five places with random effects

CIs it just the map?

Whether a person is found depends on the person, not on the place.

Across 82 matched conditions that exist in all five places, which place a person stands in explains 7–15% of the variation in whether they are found. Their apparent size, how much of them is hidden, the shape of what is left and how many pixels remain explain 84–88% together.

LimitFive places, all built by us, with one engine and one pipeline. Going from three places to five raised the place’s share for one of the two detectors, to 15% when weighted by people. The plan is ten, and the question stays open until then.

Measured 1 October 2026 · YOLO11m and RT-DETR-L with public weights · cell-level recall over 82 configurations of size × occlusion × shape × pixels left · range is across both detectors, unweighted and weighted

DWhen our frames help training

With a few hundred labelled drone frames, starting from ours finds 13–29% more people than training on them alone. With a thousand, it adds almost nothing.

One detector, starting from public weights, was fine-tuned on 250 real aerial frames: once directly, and once after first training on 2,000 of our frames. We ran it three times, with different real frames and seeds, and scored every model on thousands of real frames none of them had seen, holding the false alarms equal. Starting from our frames found more people every time. With 500 or 1,000 real frames, the gain shrank to about 4%, inside the measurement’s margin.

+13–29%

+4%

LimitOur frames do not replace real ones: trained on ours alone, the detector finds fewer people on real footage than one taken off the shelf. Above about 500 real frames, training longer on your own data matters more than adding ours. One real dataset, one detector family, people only; the 500- and 1,000-frame results are single runs, and three repeats are still few.

Measured 1–2 October 2026 · YOLO11m from public weights · VisDrone-DET-train frames held out from every model: 6,221 per repeat, 5,471 for the single runs · one false alarm per frame

06

How we work

  1. Exact means read from the simulator.

    Nothing is drawn by hand, and every field on every frame says whether it is exact, measured, a proxy or provenance.

  2. A check can answer “inconclusive”.

    When nothing measured what a check exists to measure, it says so instead of passing.

  3. Corrections stay on the record.

    Our occlusion-shape result shrank from 4.2–8.5× to 2.0× once we held the pixels left constant, and again when most of the 2.0× turned out to be the box, not the detection; on real footage it measures 1.2×. Our agreement with real footage read +0.98 until “hidden” was measured the same way on both sides; it is +0.86 to +0.96. The smaller numbers are the ones on this page.

  4. Every number has a source.

    Each figure here traces to a dated measurement, and carries the limit that comes with it.

  5. The people are rendered.

    No real person’s likeness, location or movement is recorded in our frames.

  6. Built for finding people.

    Small, partly hidden, seen at an angle, in poor light: the conditions that decide whether someone on the ground is found. This is not an operational endorsement, and nothing here has been tested in a live rescue.
